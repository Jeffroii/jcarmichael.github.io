RIT Baja SAE Brake Rotor Design

Designed brake rotors for RIT Baja's 2024 and 2026 competition vehicles, targeting an aggressive reduction in mass moment of inertia to improve rotational acceleration while meeting a secondary lightweighting goal. Working within manufacturing constraints tied to sponsor availability for laser cutting or waterjetting, I selected 4130 sheet steel and iterated rotor geometry using a simplified FEA, validated against hand-calculated maximum clamping force of brake pads derived from theoretical stopping requirements. For 2026, I developed two distinct rotor designs to accommodate different front and rear packaging constraints. The 2024 design achieved a 21.6% reduction in mass moment of inertia versus the prior year's rotor, and track testing confirmed one geometry offered a meaningful strength advantage over the other, informing which design was carried forward.

RIT Baja SAE Rotor Hub Design

Designed a new rotor hub for RIT Baja's 2026 vehicle after a redesigned brake package shrank available packaging space, requiring a hub that could clamp the rotor securely while maintaining axial positional rigidity. Working directly alongside the rotor I designed for the same subsystem, I developed a clamping interface that met the tighter space claim, avoided interference with surrounding brake components, and remained easily serviceable while the car was on-track. The result was a compact, rigid hub-and-rotor assembly that satisfied the year's tighter packaging constraints without sacrificing brake performance or maintainability.

Baja SAE Suspension Kinematics Visualization Tool

When RIT Baja lost its license for Altair MotionView, the suspension-visualization software the team had relied on, I partnered with the team's vehicle dynamics lead to extend his MATLAB code, which computed 3D suspension linkage motion but only output numerical camber and toe curves. After studying vehicle dynamics fundamentals to understand target suspension behavior, I built an interactive 3D animation that plotted the suspension linkages in space over each step of a travel loop, synced alongside the corresponding camber and toe curves. The plots could be exported as video and GIF files, giving the team a visual tool to communicate suspension behavior during internal design reviews, sponsor presentations, and competition design judging.

MATLAB Air Hockey Game

Vacuum Cannon Velocity Modeling

For RIT’s Engineering Applications Lab (MECE-301), I modeled and tested a vacuum cannon that launched a ping-pong ball through a PVC tube using a pressure differential. I developed MATLAB calculations for an iterative theoretical model, compared predicted exit velocity against experimental trials, and helped validate results using a ballistic block and Vernier ultrasonic sensor to back-calculate launch speed from block displacement. Although leakage, tape-rupture behavior, ball fit, and sensor alignment introduced uncertainty, most experimental data fell within the model’s predicted bounds, giving me practical experience connecting dynamics, fluid effects, and measurement error.

Double Pendulum Motion Analysis

For RIT’s Engineering Applications Lab (MECE-301), I analyzed a double pendulum system to compare a Lagrangian mechanics model against physical motion data. I implemented the coupled equations of motion in MATLAB/Simulink, ran a Monte Carlo uncertainty study to show sensitivity to initial angle, geometry, and material properties, and helped validate the model with a custom 80/20 test fixture, 240-fps video capture, and Kinovea motion tracking. Although the experimental curves did not fully validate the theoretical bounds, they followed similar angular-displacement trends and highlighted how chaotic systems punish small modeling and measurement errors.

Black Box System Identification

In RIT's Systems Dynamics course (MECE-320), I characterized the dynamic behavior of an unknown electrical "black box" system using only its input-output response. I drove the system across 65 frequencies and ran a step-response test, then derived a transfer function through frequency- and time-domain Bode and curve-fit analysis in MATLAB, cross-validating results against hand calculations. The two independent methods agreed within 4–14% across all poles and zeros, identifying the system as a critically damped, second-order system.

DC Servomotor Position Control

For RIT's Classical Control Systems course (MECE-643), I designed and implemented a frequency-domain lead compensator to control the angular position of a DC servomotor, driven by potentiometer feedback. Using hand-calculated Bode analysis and MATLAB, I derived a lead controller meeting target overshoot, ramp-error, and voltage-saturation specifications, then implemented it via LabVIEW to drive the physical motor at 90° and 180° step commands. While my nonlinear Simulink model struggled to predict real hardware behavior - a limitation traced to unmodeled saturation and dead-zone effects - the LabVIEW-implemented controller met the design specifications experimentally, achieving low error and no overshoot.

Embedded DC Motor Velocity Control

In RIT's Digital Control Systems course (MECE-743), I identified, modeled, and controlled the velocity response of a DC servomotor running in closed-loop using an Arduino MEGA microcontroller and MATLAB. I collected open-loop data at 20 [Hz] and 200 [Hz] sampling rates to identify first- and second-order discrete-time ARX transfer function models, then designed a PI controller via root-locus analysis to achieve a critically damped response to a 1000-2000 [RPM] step. Implemented and tested on hardware, the 200 [Hz] second-order model produced the closest match to experimental behavior, though final controller gains required manual tuning below their theoretical values to account for real-world nonlinearities like motor dead zone and saturation.

Bit Serial Adder-Subtractor FSM

For RIT’s Digital Systems I course (EEEE-120), I designed and validated a 5-bit bit-serial adder-subtractor that reused a single full-adder across multiple clock cycles to perform binary addition and two’s-complement subtraction. I built the system hierarchically in Quartus from D flip-flops, multiplexers, combinational logic, X/Y shift registers, a serial adder, and a Moore-type control FSM derived from a state diagram, state table, Gray-coded assignments, and Karnaugh maps. I verified the integrated circuit in ModelSim using the supplied testbench, confirming correct back-to-back addition/subtraction behavior.

Thermal System Modeling

For RIT's Systems Modeling course (MECE-606), I developed four thermal models of increasing order and complexity for ceramic and Styrofoam coffee cups, using first-principles heat transfer to estimate each material's external convection coefficient from a cooling dataset. Using Biot number analysis to justify each model's lumped-mass assumptions, I built single-state, two-state, N-state discretized, and Simscape representations, then validated all four against an independent warming dataset. All models produced physically reasonable convection coefficients (about 14.5 [W/m²K] ceramic, 23.2 [W/m²K] Styrofoam), with the simplest lumped model performing comparably to the higher-order alternatives.

Two-Tank Hydraulic System Modeling

For RIT's Systems Modeling course (MECE-606), I derived and validated three first-principles dynamic models of a two-tank hydraulic system, each representing orifice flow differently (laminar, Bernoulli, and empirical). I used tank draining data to identify flow parameters for each model - via time-constant analysis, parameter-sweep optimization, and MATLAB's fminsearch, respectively - and calibrated a static pump gain from separate filling data. While the empirical model best fit the individual draining datasets, the physics-based Bernoulli model generalized best to an independent full-system validation dataset, highlighting the tradeoff between curve-fitting accuracy and predictive robustness.
